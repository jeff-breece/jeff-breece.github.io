# Blog voice

Calibration notes for prose with a reader. The rules below came out of a real
editing corpus: someone recording, edit by edit, what an editor named **Allen**
changed in AI-assisted drafts. They are here because the pattern they describe
is general, and because they are unusually specific about *what an AI draft does
wrong* rather than about style in the abstract.

## Before anything else: whose voice this is

**The rules are operative. The examples are somebody else's biography.**

Every example below is a real edit to **Allen's** writing, and Allen's life is
in them: a PM background, a consulting portfolio being built up, a partner who
passes on links, opinions about OpenClaw and Cowork and Slack, a six-level
maturity framework, "tinkerers" and "L2". None of that is Jeff's.

So when the rules say *"be concrete and personal"* or *"understate
credentials"*, apply the **rule** and reach for **Jeff's** specifics. Never
carry across:

- Allen's credentials, roles or history
- Allen's projects, clients, tools or frameworks
- Allen's family, relationships or offhand attributions ("h/t my partner")
- Allen's stated opinions as though they were positions Jeff holds

If a draft needs a concrete first-person detail and there isn't one to hand,
**ask for it**. Inventing one is worse than a vague sentence: a vague sentence
reads flat, a fabricated one reads confident and is false.

## How to use this

Two modes, and they are different jobs.

**Drafting.** Write to the rules from the start. The biggest wins are up front:
no em-dashes, no X-not-Y opener, qualifiers on flat claims, and a concrete
first-person anchor early.

**Editing a draft.** Go looking for the tells in roughly this order, because
this is roughly how often they show up:

1. Em-dashes. Mechanical, so do it first and get it out of the way.
2. Absolutes stated flatly. Qualify nearly all of them.
3. Intensifier adverbs, "genuinely" above all.
4. Rhetorical moves: X-not-Y, quantified punchlines, rule-of-three, metaphor
   verbs, comparative tails, punchy paragraph closers.
5. Abstract characterization where an observable detail would do.
6. Section-opener signposts.

When you cut a rhetorical beat, **replace it with a sentence that explains the
mechanism**. A deletion leaves a hole; the fix is prose that says what is
actually happening.

## What a good pass looks like

Cutting alone makes prose thinner, not more human. The corpus shows the editor
*adding* as much as removing: loose connectors, dry parentheticals, an
exclamation point, a specific number, an analogy, a self-aware caveat before a
sharp claim. A draft that has only been trimmed still reads as AI output with
fewer words.

# The rules

*Preserved as written. "Allen" throughout means the editor whose corpus these
came from - see the provenance note above before importing any of his
specifics.*

## Voice and confidence calibration

**Soften absolutes pervasively.** This is the single most common edit. Add qualifiers ("often", "usually", "generally", "probably", "might", "can") at nearly every claim stated flatly. Examples from past edits: "the technical challenge is smaller" → "is *often* smaller"; "the backlog is almost entirely edge cases" → "*can fill up quickly* with..."; "Prioritization gets muddled" → "Prioritization *often* feels muddled"; "won't get the same result" → "*might* not get the same result"; "it's a real differentiator" → "*can be* a real differentiator"; "the playbook is different" → "the playbook is *slightly* different". Even tiny softenings count: "is close to the opposite" → "is *closer* to the opposite"; "they do it through Slack" → "they do *a lot of* it through Slack".

**Avoid intensifier adverbs.** "Genuinely" (chief offender), "truly", "really", "actually" (when used for emphasis, not to mean "in fact"). These are tells of trying to push a claim harder than the underlying observation supports. Allen edits them out almost universally. Examples from past edits: "is *genuinely* the right tool" → "feels like the right tool"; "feels *genuinely* closer to a person" → "feels close to a person"; "the parts where parsing *genuinely* needs judgment" → "the parts where parsing needs judgment".

**Reject the clever/punchy moves.** AI drafts reach for rhetorical compression; Allen edits it back to plain. Specific patterns to avoid:

- X-not-Y parallel constructions ("isn't ten things, it's a thousand" rejected for "is filled with tons of legitimate edge cases"). **Hot zone: opening sentences.** Drafts often open with X-not-Y ("what's surprised me most isn't how it works. It's how few things..."). When you spot it in an opener, rewrite as a single direct statement.

- Quantified punchlines ("three bullets instead of fifteen" rejected for plain description)

- Rhetorical rule-of-three / parallel triples, especially negations ("no install, no new tab, no new habit to build" rejected for the plain "The new workflow works itself into existing workflows"). Three parallel items built for rhythm rather than information get flattened to one plain statement.

- Metaphor-loaded verbs and nouns ("becomes load-bearing" → "becomes noticeably missing"; "users route around it" → "users politely ignore the feature"; "OpenClaw's *just figure it out* posture earns its keep" → "OpenClaw's *just figure it out* approach is very effective"; "a sharper map of where agents like this fit" → "heightened my sense of how OpenClaw compares to other agents"). The noun version is just as bad as the verb version. This applies to connective tissue, not just key claims: "held Tobi's framing up against what I was actually building" → "thought about Tobi's framing". Invented compact coinages get flattened the same way: "a tab-flick to something already running" → "it's already become a habit".

- Emotional adjectives where factual ones work ("awful to spec precisely" → "time-consuming to spec precisely"). Replace effortful-sounding adjectives with plain factual ones.

- Comparative tail-ends ("more X than anything else I use", "feels closer to a person than to anything else I use"). The comparative usually adds nothing. Cut everything from "than" onward.

- Summary restatements that land a beat ("They're collaborators, not overlapping" cut entirely)

- Melodramatic asides ("burning out quietly" cut entirely)

- Rhetorical three-beat closings ("It's the liaison." cut entirely)

**When you cut a rhetorical beat, replace it with prose that explains the mechanism.** Deleting a punchy line isn't enough on its own; the strongest replacement is a sentence that says what's *actually happening*. Example from a LinkedIn edit: "companies get to L2 and *stall*. The tinkerers stay tinkerers. Everyone else stays watching." → "many companies have gotten to L2, but haven't figured out how to proceed further yet. Tinkerers are tinkering and coming up with cool stuff, but neither their creations nor that mindset are spreading quickly across the organization." The X-stays-X callback got replaced by a sentence naming the two specific things that aren't spreading (the creations, and the mindset).

**Prefer concrete observable detail over abstract characterization.** When describing behavior, name what's visible, not what it produces. Examples: "who come back from engineer conversations with cleaner specs" (abstract output) → "who enjoy talking to engineers" (observable behavior); SMEs "are stuck" (abstract state) → "they don't know quite how to act or how hard to push for things with the engineers" (actual experience); "The underwriter sits next to the engineer" (visual) → "The engineer goes to ask the underwriter questions as they go" (actual interaction pattern).

**A coined concept is fine if you ground it with concrete detail right beside it.** "Prefer concrete over abstract" doesn't mean never naming things. Allen will *add* a conceptual label, then anchor it. Example from a LinkedIn edit: "they're the ones taking real chunks of work off their own plate" → "the tinkerers are the ones doing *internal innovation*, taking real chunks of work off their own plate." He added the abstraction ("internal innovation") and kept the concrete grounding in the same sentence.

**Be concrete and personal with claims, not vague.** Don't write "made it possible to build things I couldn't have built" when "made me literally more than 10x more efficient" is available. Use specific, first-person claims over hedged generalities.

**Pair credibility claims with specific data or concrete failure modes.** When asserting something works or fails, attach a number or specific behaviors. Not "it works pretty well" but "it has only hallucinated once out of a couple hundred companies, and it was pretty obvious." Not "it just wasn't reliable enough" but "scheduled jobs would fail, it wouldn't follow long prompts exactly, and it would hallucinate URLs." Allen will add this kind of texture in editing if the draft doesn't have it.

**Use analogies, especially when explaining what something IS or in closings.** Allen reaches for analogies (e.g. "personal assistant" for AI tools, "precocious, proactive college intern" for OpenClaw) because they collapse complexity for the reader. Abstract framings like "precision tool", "all-purpose agent", or "Swiss Army knife" usually get edited into a concrete real-world analogy.

**Ground topical claims in current personal context.** When introducing an example or use case, anchor it to what Allen is actually doing right now, not a generic role description. E.g. "I'm a consultant, so a typical example..." → "Right now, I'm building up my own consulting portfolio, so here's a typical example..."

**Add early hedging caveats before strong claims.** When making a sharp observation, ground it first with a self-aware softener. Example Allen added in editing: opening with "the initial magic has faded", then immediately "Don't get me wrong: I still think it's pretty darn cool and occasionally have a 'wow' moment with it." Then the actual point.

**Disambiguate pronouns when multiple plausible referents are in scope.** When writing about Claude tools (Claude, Cowork, Code, OpenClaw, ChatGPT, etc.), watch for "it" being ambiguous between two products. Example: "I still can't fully tell when it'll be able to open a given URL" - ambiguous between OpenClaw and Claude. Allen will replace "it'll" with "Claude will" in editing.

**Prefer first-person active framing for personal setups.** "I have OpenClaw running" beats "It's OpenClaw running." "I created a Slack workspace just for interacting with it" beats "There's a Slack workspace." Allen's voice claims ownership of his setup; depersonal framing reads more clinical.

**Prefer explicit first-person stance over impersonal evaluation.** When assessing an idea, say "I agree / I think / I found" rather than reaching for an impersonal evaluative verb. Examples from edits: "because a lot of it holds up" → "because again, I agree with a lot of it"; "the learning-in-public argument deserves its own look" → "I found it partially persuasive, but I think its effectiveness is overstated".

**Understate credentials.** Don't flex titles. "I was a PM" beats "I was Head of Product" even when the latter is accurate.

**Add personality through dry parenthetical asides.** Small humor or offhand remarks show personality without breaking flow. Examples Allen added: "(super scientifically)" when naming a concept; "I half-jokingly call this a *magical incantations* problem" when coining a term; "(h/t my partner for telling me about that last one)"; "and that's ok too" at the end of a caveat; "(and if you're none of these... well, that's not good.)".

**Casual enthusiasm is allowed - exclamation points and "this would be cool" framing.** Allen's voice isn't uniformly measured. He'll add an exclamation mark or overt enthusiasm, especially about things he's personally building or future possibilities he likes. Examples from edits: "I'm still building Slack integrations." → "I'm still building Slack integrations!"; adding "It would be awesome, for example, if Slack really leaned in this direction...".

**Replace meta-commentary about your own thinking with identity markers.** Don't write "This is the part I find myself thinking about most" or "I keep coming back to this question." Instead, ground the reader in the author's role/identity: "This is where the PM hat is useful", "as a pragmatist, I think this is overstated but perhaps directionally correct."

**Use smaller, more casual verbs.** "Wrote a recent piece" beats "published a piece." "Covered above" beats "as addressed earlier." "Gets harvested" beats "is harvested." When the polished verb feels formal, swap for the casual cousin.

## Structure and pacing

**Cut section-opener signposts.** Avoid "Two things to flag", "A few things worth paying attention to", etc. Open the section with the content, not with a preview of it.

**Don't promise a payoff the next lines don't deliver.** A clause that previews something creates an expectation the immediately following content has to honor. Example from a LinkedIn edit: "My version has six levels, built for existing pre-LLM companies, *and each one comes with its own mindset*:" was followed by a list showing only capabilities, not mindsets, so Allen cut the promise. He re-used the mindset idea later, in a spot where the content actually paid it off.

**Trim the over-explained.** If a concept was covered in a prior article, one clause or parenthetical is enough. Don't re-argue it.

**Cut redundant enumeration.** If the point is clear from one or two examples, don't list four.

**When listing 3+ related items, look for a thematic merge.** Allen often combines related sub-categories into one named bucket rather than enumerate each separately. Example: "Spinning up entire apps end-to-end" + "Reliable workflow automations" + "Google Ads management" merged into one bullet labeled **Entrepreneurship-in-a-box**. The umbrella label adds insight; the separate bullets just enumerate.

**Keep loose connectors and throat-clearers.** "Generally speaking", "But you probably don't need", "Pulling back", "By the way", "I'll flag this briefly" - these conversational transitions look like things to polish out, but they're load-bearing. Removing them is what makes the prose tip into AI-tell territory.

**Don't enforce parallel structure when speech wouldn't.** A list-like construction can break parallel and still read well, often better. Example: "Who the customers, clients, and distribution partners are, what their preferences are, how the company reaches and serves them, what they've said and done, etc." The grammar isn't strictly parallel; it sounds spoken.

**Prefer "etc." and looser enumeration over closed lists.** "X, Y, Z, etc." beats "X, Y, and Z." Closed lists feel completed and final; trailing-off feels like ongoing thought.

## Content and argumentation

**Add the qualifying nuance that sharpens the argument.** Don't just argue against something; be precise about exactly what you're arguing against. E.g. Allen added: "A product that has a context moat surrounding or contributing to a *different* core value proposition is more defensible; a standalone context moat isn't."

**Anchor an abstract noun to its concrete referent.** When a sentence leans on an abstract noun, spell out what it concretely points to, often with a hyphen-with-spaces aside. Examples from edits: "removes a tax that most organizations pay" → "removes a tax around information gating that most organizations pay"; "It produces more noise" → "It produces more noise - both in the sense of more information to take in and in the sense of people creating their own narratives -".

**State the scope of a claim out loud when it's wider than the reader might assume.** If an observation applies more broadly than the setup implies, say so explicitly rather than leaving it inferred. Example from a LinkedIn edit: Allen added "They exist in companies of any industry" to a paragraph about tinkerers, to head off the assumption that this was a tech-company-only phenomenon.

**Leave room for Allen's specific observations.** Don't fill every argument to completion. Allen will add personal reactions and first-person takes (e.g. "it feels immediately unnatural for me to go to tool X...", "h/t my partner for telling me about that last one") that make the writing his.

**Don't re-state agreement with the premise at the end.** If you've argued your position throughout the piece, trust the reader to know where you stand.

**Don't end paragraphs on manufactured punchlines.** Closing a paragraph with a tighter, punchier line than the rest of the paragraph (e.g. "The discipline isn't free.", "Snapshots are what we've always had.") usually means the punchline was added for effect. Often the right paragraph ending is the second-to-last sentence.

**End a section on a constructive, practical note - not a pointed or cynical one.** When a section builds to a critique, Allen lands it on something the reader can do or consider, not on an ironic or fatalistic observation. Example from an edit: a section ending on "the benefit accrues mostly to the people who already have time to browse, who often aren't the ones who would benefit most" (a cynical tail) → "you might want to shape the learning environment even more... this might point towards leaning on AI in private channels, as a middle ground".

**When gesturing at what could come next, name a specific idea - don't stay abstract.** Forward-looking passages ("what would it take for X to be great") shouldn't end on an abstract wish. Allen adds a concrete, picturable example of the thing he's imagining. Example from an edit: a paragraph about what Slack could do, where Allen added "It would be awesome, for example, if Slack really leaned in this direction and let developers build mini-apps with their own rich UXs that could embed into a Slack DM or channel".

## Formatting (cross-cutting)

**No em-dashes.** Use parentheses, comma-separated clauses, hyphen-with-spaces ( - ), or "e.g." / "i.e." lists instead. Em-dashes are the easiest AI tell to leave behind, so this rule is strict.

**Hyphen-with-spaces ( - ) is a valid em-dash replacement alongside parens.** Since em-dashes are out, the natural replacements are parens, comma-clauses, AND a hyphen with spaces around it. Examples from Allen's edits: "(which has only happened once out of a couple hundred companies - and it was pretty obvious)"; "There are already startups out there - not to mention Nemoclaw by Nvidia - that exist precisely to solve that gap." A hyphen-with-spaces feels like a spoken pause, slightly looser than parens. It's part of Allen's natural punctuation.
